A Pacific climate data story

Living with a Changing Climate Across the Pacific

The places in this story produced 0.0454% of world territorial CO₂ in 2023. Their ocean is warming, coral heat is spreading and a single cyclone can shake an entire national economy.

01

The Pacific share almost disappears

The 17 Pacific places shown here produced 17.3 million tonnes of territorial CO₂ in 2023. The world produced 38.1 billion tonnes. Put them on the same scale and the Pacific share is a sliver: 0.0454%, or roughly one tonne in every 2,200.

02

One cyclone can shake an entire economy

The scale changes when a cyclone reaches land. Across these four storms, assessments placed the economic blow between more than one fifth and nearly two thirds of annual GDP. Pam displaced 65,000 people in Vanuatu. Winston affected 540,400 people in Fiji.

03

Four storms sit inside a much larger web

From 1980 to 2023, 508 recorded storm tracks crossed the South Pacific. Of those, 103 reached Category 3 or above, about one in five. Scroll through the map to see Pam, Winston, Gita and Harold emerge from that wider record.

04

The slower change reaches every place

Cyclones mark individual days. Ocean warming unfolds across decades. The sea surface average for 2014 to 2023 was warmer than the 1993 to 2002 average in every one of the 21 places. French Polynesia records the smallest shift at 0.21°C. Nauru records the largest at 0.49°C. Every row moves in the same direction.

05

The heat reaches the reefs

Coral heat stress builds as unusually warm water lingers. In 2024, all eight stations crossed 4°C weeks, when significant bleaching can occur. Seven also crossed 8°C weeks, when severe bleaching and coral mortality become likely.

06

By 2023, every row ends in a higher sea level band

The early years are filled with neutral bands and a few lower readings. Warmer colours spread across the chart over time. In 2023, all 21 records show a positive anomaly. Twenty sit at +0.1 metre and Vanuatu at +0.2 metre.

07

Do wetter years bring better harvests?

The 450 observations give a messy answer. Wetter years include 124 yield gains and 105 losses. Drier years include 121 gains and 100 losses. The overall correlation is 0.04, so the points settle into a cloud instead of a clear slope.

08

The move to renewable electricity is uneven

Tokelau sits at one end of the chart, with renewables supplying 94.4% of its electricity in 2023. Fiji is the only other place above halfway, at 52.1%. The next highest share is Papua New Guinea at 33.5%, and four places remain below 10%.

The full picture

So where does that leave the Pacific?

The story began with a sliver of world emissions. It moved through storms, warmer seas, stressed reefs, changing sea levels and unpredictable harvests. The final chart shows action already under way, with plenty of distance left to travel.

Notes on the numbers

The emissions comparison uses territorial CO₂ for 2023 from one Our World in Data release. It covers the 17 Pacific places with a value for that year. Five places in the project list had no 2023 value. Consumption emissions, land use change and other greenhouse gases sit outside this measure.

Each cyclone assessment uses its own terms, so affected people, displaced people, damage and wider economic effects remain separate. The track map uses the USA Saffir Simpson category in IBTrACS and begins in 1980, within the modern satellite era. A study of cyclone trends would need its own analysis.

Degree Heating Weeks is a satellite measure of accumulated heat stress. Observations at the reef provide evidence of bleaching. Sea level values arrive rounded to 0.1 metre, while local shorelines also respond to waves, storms, sediment and land movement.

The electricity chart covers generation. Transport and other energy uses are recorded separately. The reproducible tidyverse data build is in r/build_story.R. Derived tables are stored in the story folder. Sources were accessed on 9 August 2026.
