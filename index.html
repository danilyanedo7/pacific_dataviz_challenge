A Pacific climate data story

Living with a Changing Climate Across the Pacific

Pacific emissions add up to a small part of the global total. The ocean around the islands is warming anyway. The consequences arrive through reefs, coastlines, harvests and storms.

0.0454% of global territorial CO₂ came from 17 Pacific island countries and territories with OWID coverage in 2023
540,400 people were affected by Cyclone Winston in Fiji in 2016 according to the event assessment

These two numbers do not prove that one caused the other. They show the difference in scale between contribution and exposure.

Living with a Changing Climate Across the Pacific

01

A small share can contain a wide range

Taken together, the 17 Pacific entities covered by Our World in Data produced about 17.3 million tonnes of territorial carbon dioxide in 2023. That was 0.0454 percent of the world total.

The number is small at global scale. It does not make every Pacific emitter small. The official Pacific greenhouse gas indicator spans 0.1 to 86.7 tonnes per person in 2024. Palau and New Caledonia sit far from the regional cluster.

Keep the measures apart

Our World in Data reports territorial CO₂. The Pacific dataset reports greenhouse gas emissions. They answer related questions but are not interchangeable, so the visual keeps them on separate scales.

02

Every complete ocean record moved warmer

All 21 complete sea surface temperature records were warmer on average in 2014 through 2023 than in 1993 through 2002. The shift ranges from 0.21°C in French Polynesia to 0.49°C in Nauru.

The direction is shared. The size and timing are not. Each row below compares the same two ten year windows without turning the countries into one regional average.

03

The tracks were already here

IBTrACS contains 508 South Pacific storm tracks from 1980 through 2023. A track map can show where storms travelled and which ones reached high recorded intensity. It cannot prove that warming caused an individual storm.

04

Heat becomes stress when it stays

Degree Heating Weeks accumulate coral heat stress over twelve weeks. NOAA marks 4°C weeks as a level where significant bleaching can occur. At 8°C weeks severe bleaching and mortality become likely.

The calendar shows the maximum annual value at eight Pacific regional virtual stations. It does not measure observed bleaching on the reef.

A heat stress measure

DHW combines intensity and duration. A pale year can still contain short warm periods. A dark year means stress accumulated for long enough to cross the published thresholds.

05

Annual rainfall alone does not explain harvests

Across 450 paired country years in 15 places, the correlation between annual rainfall anomaly and annual crop yield change is 0.04. That is close to no linear association at this scale.

This does not show that rainfall is unimportant. It shows that one national annual rainfall number is a poor explanation for harvest change on its own.

06

The sea level record moves into higher bands

The official values are stored in 0.1 metre steps. The visual therefore uses five broad bands rather than drawing a falsely precise line.

Most country records occupy the 0.1 metre band in recent years. These national ocean averages do not show a shoreline moving inland. Waves, sediment, engineering and land motion also change coastlines.

07

One storm can touch most of a country

Event assessments describe named cyclones. The official Pacific tables report annual totals compiled through disaster monitoring systems. The scopes differ and the numbers do not always agree.

That disagreement matters. The official annual affected series records zero for Vanuatu in 2015 while the Pam assessment documents 65,000 people displaced. Zero in the table is a reporting problem, not evidence that nobody was affected.

08

Electricity systems are changing at different speeds

In 2023, renewable sources supplied 94.4 percent of recorded electricity generation in Tokelau and 52.1 percent in Fiji. The other 16 places in the disaggregated power dataset remained below half.

Electricity is only part of energy use. Transport fuels and other final energy demand sit outside this chart. The separate SDG measure of renewable final energy is shown in the details where it is available.

What the evidence can carry

The imbalance is visible. The causal chain still needs care.

Pacific contributions to global territorial CO₂ are small in total. Ocean warming appears across every complete record. Cyclone tracks and reef heat stress show where exposure takes shape. Event reports show how large the consequences can become. Power data show that change is underway.

No single chart proves that a specific storm, harvest loss or coastline change came from climate change. The story becomes stronger when it says exactly what each dataset can support.
